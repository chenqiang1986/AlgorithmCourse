# Lesson 6.1: Generalized Addition and the Commutative Law
*Fundamental Math / Course 2*

Before introducing letters in Lesson 7, this lesson practices a powerful habit with numbers
only: turn every subtraction into addition of a negative number, then rearrange the addends
to make an equivalent expression that is easier to read or use. We are **transforming** an
expression, not finding its final numerical value.

## 1. Subtraction Is Addition of a Negative Number

Every subtraction can be rewritten without a subtraction sign:

$$7-12=7+(-12)$$

The value is unchanged. The second form makes the signed number $-12$ visible as one whole
addend. Read $7-12+3-5$ as four addends:

$$7+(-12)+3+(-5)$$

## 2. Generalized Addition

With addition, addends may be regrouped or rearranged without changing the value. This is the
**generalized addition rule**. It applies even when some addends are negative.

$$8+(-3)+5+(-10)=5+8+(-10)+(-3)$$

Only the order and grouping changed; the four numbers did not.

## 3. A Number Travels With Its Sign

Before changing an order, treat each signed number as one addend. In

$$1-2+3-4$$

the four addends are $1$, $-2$, $3$, and $-4$ — not $1$, $2$, $3$, and $4$ with signs that
can be left behind. Once each sign is attached to its number, the addends can be switched in
any order:

$$1-2+3-4=1+3-2-4=1-4-2+3$$

Nothing was calculated in this chain. The second expression has the signed addends $1$, $3$,
$-2$, and $-4$; the third has $1$, $-4$, $-2$, and $3$. Each uses exactly the same four signed
addends, merely in a different order.

Writing every subtraction as addition makes the sign-travel rule even easier to see:

$$1+(-2)+3+(-4)=1+3+(-2)+(-4)=1+(-4)+(-2)+3$$

## 4. The Commutative Law as a Rearranging Tool

The **commutative law of addition** says that two addends can trade places. Repeating that
move lets us place useful partners together:

$$4+9+(-4)+(-9)=\bigl(4+(-4)\bigr)+\bigl(9+(-9)\bigr)$$

This is especially useful when an expression contains opposite numbers. The goal is not to
calculate; it is to make the structure clear.

## 5. Rules for This Class

When a practice problem gives a transformation constraint:

1. Keep every number from the original expression.
2. Do not introduce a new number.
3. A subtraction sign may become addition of the corresponding negative number.
4. Do not calculate a final numerical answer unless the instruction specifically asks for it.

For example, the $-2$ and $-5$ below are not new magnitudes: they are the signed forms of the
original $2$ and $5$.

## 6. Reading Example: Remove Subtraction, Then Rearrange

**Example (Only Addition).** Rewrite $1-2+3-5$ so that only addition signs are used. Use no
numbers other than $1$, $2$, $3$, and $5$.

**Solution:**

First identify the signed addends: $1$, $-2$, $3$, and $-5$. Keep each number attached to its
sign while rearranging. Writing addition of negatives makes every move visible:

$$1-2+3-5=1+3-2-5=1+(-5)+(-2)+3$$

No final value was calculated, and every magnitude came from the original expression.

$\square$

## Key Takeaways

- Replacing subtraction with addition of a negative number keeps the value unchanged.
- A number travels with its sign: treat $-2$ as one signed addend, not as a number whose
  negative sign can be left behind.
- Addition allows these signed addends to be reordered and regrouped.
- In a transformation problem, preserve the original numbers and satisfy the stated form;
  calculating a final answer is unnecessary.
